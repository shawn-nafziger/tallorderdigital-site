Redistricting moved the line

Nine states redrew their congressional district maps for 2026, a process called . Replay the 2024 presidential vote on the old and new maps, and the line for a House majority moves 1.4 points. Use the tide below to see it seat by seat.

How to read these: each district's starting point is how it voted for president in 2024, measured as the . A "swing" adds the same number of points to every district at once. That's a simulation, not a forecast: real House races don't move in lockstep, and House candidates often run ahead of or behind the top of the ticket.

Where the lines moved

Of the nine states that went through redistricting, 6 drew district maps with more Trump-won districts and 2 with more Harris-won districts; in North Carolina the count didn't change. Counting districts each candidate carried in 2024, the new district maps add 11 Trump-won districts in some states and 7 Harris-won districts in others.

Each square is a district on the 2026 lines, ranked from the most Democratic (top left) to the most Republican (bottom right). The outlined square is the 218th: whoever carries it holds the majority. At the 2024 result that's WI-1, which Trump carried by 4.6 points. Pick a state to find your own district.

Move the tide

Picture a as a tide that moves every district the same number of points. Starting from the 2024 presidential vote, Democrats would need a tide of 3.2 points to win the House on the 2024 maps. On the 2026 maps they'd need 4.6: the new lines push the majority 1.4 points further out.

The new district maps also thin out the middle. 90 districts sit within 10 points of flipping on the 2024 lines; on the 2026 lines, 76 do.

How this was measured

  • Data: 2024 presidential votes for every congressional district, calculated by The Downballot for the districts used in 2024 and for the districts used in 2026. Each state's district totals match its certified 2024 statewide count within 0.1%.
  • Margin: Harris's share of the two-party vote minus Trump's, so third-party votes are left out. Shown as a lead for whoever is ahead.
  • Swing: the same number of points added to every district's margin. A district flips when its margin crosses zero. Real elections don't swing evenly, so read the curves as "how hard is each map," not "what will happen."
  • Close seats: within 2 points of flipping at the selected swing.
  • Redrawn states: Alabama, California, Florida, Louisiana, North Carolina, Ohio, Tennessee, Texas and Utah.
