Thanksgiving Weather in Your City, Every Year Since 1863

In 328 US cities' official weather records, 1930 holds the coldest Thanksgiving in more places than any other year (58), and 2017 the warmest (53). Here is every Thanksgiving since 1863 in your city, and how this year's forecast for November 26 stacks up.

Which Thanksgivings set the most records?

1930 and 2017 set the most: 1930 holds or shares the coldest Thanksgiving high in 58 of the 328 cities, and 2017 the warmest in 53. Thanksgiving is one day, so one cold wave or warm spell can set a whole region's records at once.

  • 1930 holds or shares the coldest Thanksgiving high in 58 places, more than any other year, from the Midwest to the Carolinas.
  • 1985 holds or shares the coldest Thanksgiving high in 33 places, across the Northwest and the northern Plains.
  • 2018 is the most recent: it holds or shares the coldest Thanksgiving high in 23 places in New York, New England and Pennsylvania.
  • 2017 holds or shares the warmest in 53 places, nearly all in the West and Plains, from Southern California to the Dakotas.
  • 1941, the last Thanksgiving before the date was fixed, holds or shares the warmest Thanksgiving high in 30 places, mostly in the Northeast.

Where does it snow on Thanksgiving?

In the lower 48, Marquette, Michigan has the best odds of measurable snow on Thanksgiving: about 49% of Thanksgivings since its record began, followed by Duluth, Minnesota (34%) and Rochester, New York (33%).

The deepest Thanksgiving snow in any of these records fell on Flagstaff, Arizona: 23 inches in 1919. The biggest storm came on Thanksgiving 1971, with 21.8 inches in Albany, New York, 18.5 in Wilkes-Barre/Scranton, Pennsylvania, 18.0 in Glens Falls, New York, and 14.8 in Worcester, Massachusetts. The biggest since then: 18.8 inches in Lander, Wyoming, in 2023.

Is Thanksgiving getting warmer?

One day a year is too little to tell. A single heat wave or cold snap swings the count either way: 2017 alone holds about half of all the warmest-Thanksgiving records set since 2000. The year-round answer is clearer. Since 2000, US cities have set only about half the daily record lows chance would give. Read Record Lows Are Disappearing.

How this was measured

  • Data: the official daily record NOAA keeps for each city (ThreadEx records via the Regional Climate Centers' ACIS service), the same 328 records the Almanac publishes. Records are as kept, without adjustments.
  • Thanksgiving is the national holiday, from the first in 1863: the last Thursday of November (in 1865, the first Thursday of December), the earlier dates President Roosevelt set in 1939-1941 (about half the states kept the last Thursday those years), and the fourth Thursday since 1942.
  • Warmest and coldest compare each Thanksgiving's high temperature. When years tie, each holds the record ("holds or shares").
  • Snow odds are the share of Thanksgivings with at least 0.1 inch of snowfall, among years with a snow reading.
  • This year's forecast is the National Weather Service's, for the 53 places we track hourly, ranked only where the forecast point is the record's own site. A forecast "would be" a rank; it isn't a record until the day is over. Ranks count years strictly warmer (or colder), plus one; ties share. Outside the warmest or coldest fifth, a forecast reads as typical.
