Heat or growth? What set this summer’s power records

Three of the country’s biggest power grids hit all-time highs in summer 2026. We matched 11 summers of hourly grid data to the weather to see how much was the heat.

By Shawn Nafziger · Published Nov 24, 2026 · Demand data through November 18, 2026
Sources: U.S. Energy Information Administration, NOAA, Census Bureau

On equally hot summer weekdays, Texas’s grid needed 23% more power in 2026 than in 2019, and the Southwest Power Pool needed 10% more. That growth is what pushed both grids to new records this summer: our comparison of hourly EIA demand with NOAA temperatures covers about 930 summer weekdays per grid, 2016 to 2026.

The heat helped too. Summer 2026 was the hottest or second-hottest of the last 11 summers in both regions, and EIA called it the hottest summer on record nationally. But in Texas the record day itself was barely hotter than the last one: ERCOT peaked at 91.1 gigawatts on July 22, 2026, when the population-weighted high was 102.4°F, versus 85.5 GW on August 20, 2024, at 101.6°F.

Key facts

  • On a 100°F summer weekday, ERCOT’s modeled peak rose from 73.7 GW in 2019 to 90.5 GW in 2026 (+23%).
  • At the same temperature, 2026 peaks were up 10% in the Southwest Power Pool, 7% in PJM and 4% in MISO, flat in New England and down 6% in New York.
  • PJM, ERCOT and the Southwest Power Pool set all-time records in summer 2026 (EIA). SPP and MISO reached their highest demand on days cooler than their previous peak day.
  • Coverage: 7 grid operators serving about 220 million people; hourly data from January 2016; demand and temperature matched for 99.6% to 99.9% of hours.

How much more power does the same hot day take now?

In Texas, about a fifth more. Each dot below is one summer weekday: how hot it got across ERCOT, weighted by where people live, and the grid’s highest hour of demand that day. The 2026 days sit well above the 2019 days at every temperature.

Which grids are using more power at the same temperature?

Texas by far, then the Southwest Power Pool and PJM. California and the Midwest grew a little, New England held steady and New York used less.

When did Texas start pulling away?

In 2021, and its same-temperature demand has risen every summer since. The other grids stayed within about 5% of 2019 through 2024; SPP and PJM climbed in 2025 and 2026.

Where did the heat matter most?

On the peak days in the East, though those summers were ordinary overall. PJM’s record day was about 2°F hotter than its previous peak day, so PJM needed both the heat and the growth: its 2026 peak of 162.6 GW on July 2 beat the June 2025 record by 2.0 GW. New York’s 2026 peak day was about 5°F hotter than its August 2016 peak day, yet demand fell 1 GW short, because New York now uses less power at a given temperature than it did.

What this doesn’t tell us

It shows how much more power each grid needs at a given temperature, not why. Data centers, new factories, population growth, electric vehicles and the electrification of oil fields are all possible reasons, and this data can’t separate them. Rooftop solar lowers the demand a grid sees at midday, which is part of why California looks flat. A grid’s territory can also change over time, which would show up here as growth.

How this was measured

Demand. Hourly demand for the seven grid operators from the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s Form EIA-930, January 2016 to November 18, 2026. EIA publishes each grid’s hours as reported; we screened out missing, zero and sudden-spike hours (fewer than 0.1% of hours except SPP, 2.6%) and used EIA’s adjusted series where it filled them. Our 2026 peaks match EIA’s published records.

Temperature. Hourly readings from 198 airport weather stations in NOAA’s Global Historical Climatology Network hourly (GHCNh). Each county was assigned to its nearest station and weighted by its Census 2025 population and by the share of its electric customers each grid serves, from EIA’s Form EIA-861 (2025). That gives one temperature per grid per hour, weighted by where people live. 99.94% of the Lower 48’s population is mapped to a grid.

Model. For each grid, each summer weekday (June to September, holidays excluded) has its highest hour of demand and the day’s highest population-weighted temperature. We fit one curve shape per grid and let its level change by year (log of peak demand = year + temperature + temperature²). The year levels give the change at the same temperature; the reference hot day is each grid’s 95th-percentile summer high. Two other versions, one using only the hottest quarter of days, agree within 2 percentage points. The short lines on the grid chart show where each number would land 9 times in 10 if the summers were redrawn at random from the same days; they treat each day as independent, so the real wobble is somewhat larger.
