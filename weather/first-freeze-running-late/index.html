Fall's First Freeze Is Running Late

77 of the 213 US places whose first freeze usually comes by now are still waiting for it. In 33 of them, mostly across the Midwest and Great Lakes, a fall this slow has come about once a decade or less.

By Shawn Nafziger · Official NOAA city records, data through November 8, 2026

A third of the due places are still waiting

Every place has a typical date for its first night below freezing: the middle of its first freezes from 1991 to 2020. In Fargo it's Oct 2. In New Orleans it's Jan 3. By November 8, 213 of the 325 places in the Almanac had passed theirs. Only 136 have actually frozen.

Late across the Midwest and Great Lakes

The orange dots cluster from the Plains to the Ohio Valley. Chicago's first freeze typically comes about Oct 22. This fall its coldest night so far is 33°F. Of 154 falls on record since 1872, only 27 had gone this long without one. It's rarer still in Omaha (11 of 155 falls) and Grand Rapids (5 of 132). Detroit and Salt Lake City are on the list too.

From Fargo to New Orleans

Among the biggest cities, the first freeze runs from early October on the northern Plains to the turn of the year on the Gulf Coast. Fargo's typical date is Oct 2; this fall it came Oct 19. New Orleans waits until about Jan 3. And Phoenix has no typical date at all: its official thermometer at Sky Harbor dropped below freezing in only 5 of the 30 winters from 1991 to 2020.

What a typical date does and doesn't mean

It's a middle, not a forecast. In half of years the first freeze comes earlier, in half later. A late fall is only news when it's late against that spread, which is why the orange dots are the places where 9 falls in 10 had frozen by now.

Your yard is probably colder. These are official city thermometers, mostly at airports, which run warmer than suburbs and the countryside, especially in big cities. Gardeners a few miles out often see frost weeks earlier.

32°F is a knife edge. "Below freezing" here means a low of 31°F or colder, the same line NOAA uses for its official first-freeze dates. 13 of the 33 orange places have touched exactly 32°F this fall; count 32 as a freeze and they'd be done. Every place named above has stayed at 33°F or warmer, so it's late either way.

How this was measured

  • Data: the official daily record NOAA keeps for each city (ThreadEx records via the Regional Climate Centers' ACIS service), the same records the Almanac publishes, through November 8, 2026. Records are as kept, without adjustments; many join a downtown site and an airport.
  • First freeze: the first night from July 1 on with a low of 31°F or colder (below 32°F, in the whole degrees the records keep). This matches NOAA's 1991–2020 climate normals: for 26 big cities, our typical dates land on NOAA's published ones within a few days.
  • Typical date: the middle (median) of the first freezes of 1991–2020, counting a winter with no freeze as later than any date. A fall counts if no more than 10 nights are missing before its first freeze; a place needs 25 counted falls. Places that froze in fewer than half those winters "rarely freeze".
  • Unusually late (orange): still waiting on November 8, when 90% or more of the 1991–2020 falls had frozen by that date. Light orange: 75% to 90%. "Falls this late" counts every fall in a place's whole record that was still waiting by the same date.
  • Not included: places whose record has too few counted falls, the few the Almanac's own checks hold back, and 5 places whose record has missing nights this fall or runs a few days behind.
