Record Lows Are Disappearing

Across 328 US cities with official weather records, the cold extremes have nearly stopped. Since 2000 they've set only 51% of the daily record lows that chance alone would give. Record highs are coming faster than ever.

By Shawn Nafziger · Official NOAA city records, data through November 8, 2026

Half the cold records chance would give

Every city's record book has one record low for each calendar date: the coldest January 9, the coldest July 20, and so on. If the climate weren't changing, any year would have the same shot at holding one. A city with 150 years of records would expect each year to hold about 1 in 150 of them, and the years since 2000 to hold their fair share.

They don't. Across 328 places, the years since 2000 hold 14,031 daily record lows. If every year had the same chance, they'd hold about 27,655. The same years hold 38,281 record highs, against the same 27,655 by chance. For every record low set since 2000, about 2.7 record highs were set.

The cold went first

From the 1940s through the 1980s, record lows arrived at or above the rate chance would give. The 1970s, remembered for brutal winters, set 1.3 times as many as chance. Then the 1990s set 76% of their share, the 2000s 59% and the 2010s 52%. The 2020s so far are at 36%.

Record highs tell a different story. The 2000s actually set slightly fewer than chance (94%). The surge came later: 1.4 times chance in the 2010s and 2.0 times so far in the 2020s, beating even the Dust Bowl 1930s (1.5), the old high point. Cities stopped getting record cold before they started piling up record heat.

Nearly everywhere

This isn't a few places pulling the total. In 256 of the 328 places, the shortfall is too big to be luck, and 192 have set half or fewer of the record lows chance would give. Only 6 have set more than their share.

Not just big-city heat

Cities grow around their thermometers, and pavement holds heat overnight, which makes cold records harder to set. That shows: the 50 biggest metro areas have set just 33% of their share of record lows since 2000. But the other 278 places, from Hibbing to Key West, are at 53%, still about half. Every region lands between 48% and 59%, and the 207 records that began by 1900 are at 48%.

Station moves and new instruments can also bend a record, so we ran the same test on NOAA's adjusted data, which corrects for them. It averages each state's weather by month, so it asks a simpler question: which year had each state's coldest January, coldest February, and so on? Since 2000, the 48 contiguous states hold 20 of those coldest-month records. Chance alone would give about 117. 31 states have none.

The exceptions

6 places have set more record lows than chance since 2000: Austin (Bergstrom), TX; Hibbing, MN; Jacksonville, FL; Klamath Falls, OR; Burns, OR; Casper, WY. Four of them have a plain explanation: the official thermometer moved to a colder spot, and the record's nights dropped 2.7 to 3.2°F at the move.

  • Jacksonville moved from downtown to the airport in 1956.
  • Burns moved from town to its airport around 1980.
  • Austin's Bergstrom record turned colder around 1998, as the former Air Force base became the city's airport.
  • Klamath Falls moved from a site in town to the airport in 2001.

A colder site sets cold records it couldn't have set before. Count only the years since the move, and Jacksonville, Burns and Bergstrom have set about the share of record lows chance would give, or fewer. (Klamath Falls moved too recently to check that way.) Hibbing and Casper are still a little above chance even counted from the 1960s, and we found no station change that explains them.

Why it matters

A record low isn't just a number. Hard freezes kill pests and set the length of the growing season; cold snaps are what pipes, crops and power grids are built to survive. A record book that has stopped adding cold entries is one of the plainest signs of a warming climate, visible in each city's own thermometer.

Others have found the same pattern with different methods. A 2009 study led by the National Center for Atmospheric Research, using about 1,800 quality-checked US stations, found record highs outnumbering record lows about two to one in the 2000s (NCAR). This story counts records that still stand rather than records as they were set, so the numbers differ, but the direction is the same.

The heat records get the headlines. The quieter change is the cold ones that no longer come.

How this was measured

  • Data: the official daily record NOAA keeps for each city (ThreadEx records via the Regional Climate Centers' ACIS service), the same 328 records the Almanac publishes. Records are as kept, without adjustments; many join a downtown site and an airport.
  • A record low is the coldest low for a calendar date that still stands today. When years tie, they share it. February 29 is left out.
  • "By chance" gives every year with a reading on that date the same share of that date's record, then adds the shares up. It's the count you'd expect if the climate were steady.
  • "Clearly fewer": for each place, the same number of years was drawn at random from its own record 20,000 times (whole years, so one cold snap's records stay together). Fewer than 2.5% of draws doing as well counts as clear; under 6% is "at the edge".
  • Groups: the 50 biggest metro areas are the Almanac's first wave, one official record each. Regions follow state lines.
  • Adjusted data: NOAA's nClimDiv statewide monthly averages of daily lows (October 2026 release), corrected for station moves, instrument and observing-time changes. Same test: each state and calendar month's coldest year since 1895, ties shared.
  • Not included: 5 places the Almanac's own checks hold back (too few complete years, or checks that disagree about nights).
