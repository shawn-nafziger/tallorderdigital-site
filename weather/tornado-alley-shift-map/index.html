Is Tornado Alley moving?

Partly, and not the way the headlines suggest. Among strong tornadoes (EF2 and up), the six Plains states from Texas to North Dakota went from 55 a year in 1979–93 to 36 a year in 2008–22, while the six Mid-South states from Arkansas to Kentucky went from 34 to 46 and now get more than the Plains. But the center of strong-tornado activity has barely moved east since the 1970s. Tornado Alley isn’t sliding east so much as fading in the Plains while the Mid-South takes a bigger share.

    Where is Tornado Alley?

    Every tornado since 1950, drawn along its recorded path: where paths pile up, the map glows. The brightest band runs from central Texas through Oklahoma and Kansas into Nebraska, the classic Tornado Alley. A second, more scattered glow covers Arkansas, Mississippi, Alabama and Tennessee, a region forecasters call the Mid-South or Dixie Alley. Along the Gulf and Florida’s coasts the streaks are short and dense: many small, brief tornadoes.

    Has the center moved?

    Press play to watch the decades go by: each window of ten years glows orange, earlier years fade to blue, and the white dot marks the average starting point of the tornadoes on screen. The center of all reported tornadoes sat near Springfield, Missouri, in the 1950s and moved about 70 miles east by the 2010s; in 2020–22, just 3 years so far, it sits 145 miles east of where it began. On wider screens, the box in the corner magnifies the trail.

    3 of 5

    Or are we just counting more?

    Partly. The record grew from 479 tornadoes a year in the 1950s to 1,191 in the 2010s, and almost all of the growth is weak EF0 tornadoes: 22% of the 1950s record, 52% of the 2010s. Spotter networks, Doppler radar (installed nationwide in the 1990s), storm chasers and phone cameras now catch brief tornadoes that once went unrecorded. Shown alone, the weak tornadoes trace where people and radar are as much as where storms are: the Denver suburbs, Houston, Florida’s coasts. Strong tornadoes, the ones that are hard to miss, did not increase: 189 a year in the 1950s, 135 in the 2010s.

    4 of 5

    Strong tornadoes only: does the shift hold?

    Yes for the balance, no for the center. Comparing 1979–93 with 2008–22, the Plains lost strong tornadoes (55 a year to 36, −35%) and the Mid-South gained (34 to 46, +36%); orange on the map is more strong tornadoes a year, blue is fewer. The Mid-South’s share of the two regions’ strong tornadoes rose from 38% to 56%, a trend that holds up statistically since 1973. But the average location of strong tornadoes has moved only about 5 miles east per decade since 1973, and a little south, a change too small to tell from chance, partly because the Midwest, also east of the Plains, lost strong tornadoes too (41 to 30 a year).

    What happened in your county?

    Type a county or click the map. Hinds County, Miss., home of Jackson, recorded 8 tornadoes in the 1950s, 32 in the 2010s and 14 in 2020–22 alone: 117 in all, 30 of them strong. Oklahoma County, Okla., at the heart of the old Tornado Alley, has seen 131 (47 strong, including the F5 of May 3, 1999), spread fairly evenly across the decades. Big counties collect more tornadoes simply by being big, so the card also gives a rate per 1,000 square miles.

    The strong-tornado test

    Weak tornadoes are a moving target: how many get recorded depends on who is watching. So the claim that Tornado Alley is shifting has to survive among strong tornadoes alone. It does, but only as a change in balance between regions: the Plains are losing strong tornadoes and the Mid-South is taking a growing share. The geographic center of strong tornadoes hasn’t moved east by a measurable amount since 1973, and the very strongest (EF3 and up) are too few to say either way.

    Counties with the most tornadoes since 1950

    The counties at the top of the list are big, or near radar and people, or both: Weld County, Colo., is bigger than Delaware, and Florida’s coastal counties record many brief, weak tornadoes, some of them waterspouts that move ashore. Ranked by strong tornadoes, Oklahoma County and Houston’s Harris County lead, and Alabama and Arkansas fill much of the rest of the list.

    How the tornado record changed, and how this page handles it

    The record itself. The Storm Prediction Center’s database lists every confirmed US tornado since 1950 with its date, rating, and start and end points. The national list was started in the mid-1950s and filled back to 1950 from older publications. This page uses one row per tornado (68,617 in the Lower 48; 75 in Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are left out) and draws each path as a straight line from start to end. 26,424 tornadoes have no recorded end point and appear as dots.

    More eyes, more weak tornadoes. Reports rose from about 479 a year in the 1950s to about 1,191 in the 2010s. Spotter training, the national Doppler radar network of the 1990s, storm chasing and phone video all made brief, weak tornadoes easier to catch, especially near cities. That is why this page tests the shift on strong tornadoes, which were hard to miss even in the 1950s.

    Ratings are estimates from damage. Tornadoes are rated by what they destroy, not by measured wind. A tornado crossing open country can be rated low because there was little to damage. The Fujita (F) scale was adopted gradually in the mid-1970s, and tornadoes from 1950 to the early 1970s were rated afterward, largely from newspaper accounts and photographs; many think those ratings run high, which helps explain why the 1950s through the 1970s show more strong tornadoes a year than any decade since. That is why the trends here are checked from 1973 on as well as from 1950.

    The switch from F to EF. On Feb. 1, 2007, the Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale replaced the F scale. It uses more kinds of damage (28 types of buildings and trees) and was designed so that ratings would carry over, so F2 and EF2 are treated as the same class here. Since 2016, tornadoes that left no damage to rate are recorded as EF-Unknown (756 through 2022); they count in “All” but in no rating class. In 2016 the Storm Prediction Center also estimated ratings for 1,863 tornadoes from 1953–1982 that had been listed as unknown, using damage costs and path length.

    The tests. Region counts use each tornado’s starting state. Changes compare 1979–93 (the start of the period studied by Gensini and Brooks) with the latest 15 years, 2008–22; the 95% ranges come from resampling years 4,000 times. Trends fit a straight line to each year’s value (the average starting longitude, or the Mid-South’s share) and resample years for the range. The ranges around each decade’s center resample whole tornado days, since outbreaks move together. A change whose range includes zero is reported as too small to tell from chance.

    What the data can’t say. Whether the shift is caused by a warming climate is an open research question: the environments that produce tornadoes have been shifting in the same direction, but tornado records are short and noisy. This page measures where tornadoes were recorded, not why.
