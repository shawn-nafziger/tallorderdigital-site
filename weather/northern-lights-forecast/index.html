Will you see the northern lights tonight?

The northern lights reach the northern US only during geomagnetic storms. Since 1932 there have been 3,636 strong enough (Kp 5 or more) to put the aurora low on the horizon along the Canadian border, about 38 a year, and they come in waves with the sun’s 11-year cycle: 2025 had 43, as many as any year since 2003. Only 23 reached Kp 9, the top of the scale, when the aurora can be seen as far south as Alabama or Florida; the latest was May 10–11, 2024.

The globe below is NOAA’s live aurora forecast, the chance of the aurora being overhead in the next half hour or so, refreshed every few minutes. Pick your place to see whether it reaches you tonight and how often it has since 1932.

    Can you see it from where you are?

    South Bend, IN sits at 51.1° magnetic latitude. The aurora is high in the sky, so it can be seen low on the horizon from hundreds of miles beyond the ring: the dashed gold line marks that limit, and anywhere poleward of it has a chance if the sky is dark and clear.

    What about tonight?

    From South Bend, IN you typically need a Kp of about 5, a minor geomagnetic storm, to see the aurora low on the northern horizon. The bars below the globe are NOAA’s three-day Kp forecast; the shaded columns are your dark hours. The globe now shows the ring modeled for tonight’s highest forecast Kp. Clouds and city lights matter as much as the forecast, and a phone camera on night mode often picks up a glow your eyes miss.

    How far south can it reach?

    Each step up the Kp scale pushes the aurora about 2° of magnetic latitude, roughly 140 miles, toward the equator. At Kp 9, the top of the scale, it can be overhead in the northern US and low on the horizon across the South. On May 10–11, 2024, the strongest storm in 21 years, it was photographed from Florida, Mexico and Puerto Rico. Drag the What if Kp slider to try any level.

    Each streak is one of the 3,636 geomagnetic storms since 1932, drawn down to how far south its aurora could be seen; hover or tap one for its date and reach. South Bend, IN, needs about Kp 5.0: 2,857 storms since 1932 reached that, 152 of them since 2020.

    How far south does each level of storm reach?

    NOAA rates geomagnetic storms from G1 to G5 by the Kp index, a 0–9 measure of how hard the solar wind is shaking Earth’s magnetic field, taken every three hours. Each level pushes the aurora toward the equator. These are typical limits in magnetic latitude, at the darkest hours around midnight; a storm’s peak can run past them for an hour or two.

    What Kp do you need to see the northern lights?

    It depends on your magnetic latitude, which differs from your map latitude because the magnetic pole sits in the Canadian Arctic: Seattle and London are at almost the same map latitude, but Seattle is about 5° farther north magnetically. A rule of thumb from NOAA: the aurora’s edge sits near 66° magnetic latitude when Kp is 0 and moves about 2° toward the equator for each step of Kp, and you can see it low on the horizon from about 5° beyond the edge.

    Every Kp 9 storm since 1932

    Twenty-three storms since 1932 have reached Kp 9, and 14 of them came before 1961, seven in the four years around the peak of solar cycle 19 (1957–1960). Since then there have been nine, the last two on Oct. 29–31, 2003, and May 10–11, 2024.

    Storms by solar cycle

    The sun’s activity rises and falls over about 11 years, and storms follow. Cycle 19, which peaked in 1958, was the strongest on record; cycle 24 was the weakest in a century. Cycle 25 peaked around late 2024, so big storms stay likely through 2026 and 2027 before tapering off.

    How this works, and what it can’t tell you

    The live forecast is NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center’s OVATION Prime model, which turns solar-wind measurements from satellites about a million miles upstream into the chance of visible aurora on a one-degree grid, 30 to 90 minutes ahead. Your browser downloads it directly from NOAA every five minutes, along with the latest Kp and NOAA’s three-day Kp forecast; nothing is stored.

    The horizon line on the live view follows Case, MacDonald and Viereck (2016, Space Weather), who checked NOAA’s forecast against about 500 sightings and found people often saw the aurora farther south than NOAA’s own line suggested. Their best-fitting line, used here, sits 8° of latitude plus a little more for brighter displays toward the equator from the forecast’s peak at each longitude. On the “Tonight” and “What if” views, the ring is not NOAA’s model but a simple sketch built on NOAA’s Kp rule of thumb (edge at 66° magnetic latitude minus 2° per Kp, visible about 5° beyond it), so it shows where a storm of that strength typically reaches, not a forecast.

    Magnetic latitudes come from the AACGM-v2 model at 110 km, the height of the aurora’s lower edge. Storm history is the Kp index from GFZ Potsdam, the official source since 1932; the last few weeks are preliminary. Kp tops out at 9, so the biggest storms (1989, 2003, 2024) tie on this scale even though some reached farther than “Kp 9” suggests. How far south each storm reached is estimated from its highest Kp, not from sightings, and darkness, weather and the hour of the peak decided what people actually saw.

    What it can’t tell you: whether it will be cloudy, how much light pollution you have, or exactly when a storm will arrive. Forecasts of Kp more than a day ahead are often off by a level or two, especially when a solar eruption’s arrival time is uncertain.
