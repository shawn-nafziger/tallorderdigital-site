Chance wobble

Statisticians call it the standard error

How much a percentage would move by luck alone if the same games were played again. The more shots (or games) behind a number, the smaller its wobble.

The coin-flip versionFlip a fair coin 100 times. You expect 50 heads, but you'll usually land somewhere between about 45 and 55. That ±5 is the chance wobble. About 3 times in 4 you'll land in that range. Getting 60 or more heads (twice the wobble) happens only about 1 time in 35. Getting 70 or more (four times) happens about 4 times in 100,000: essentially never by luck.
Press a button. Each flip adds a dot at the share of heads.

Teal dot: one set of flips.
Orange dot: a rare one, more than twice the wobble from 50%, by pure luck.
Shaded: the normal wobble (±1) and twice the wobble (±2) for each number of coins.
With 100 coins the wobble is ±5 points; with 1,000 coins it shrinks to about ±1.6.

In basketballOn 369 opponent shots against Miami, the share taken at the rim would normally wobble about ±2.3 points. It was 9.5 points below the league average, about four times the wobble, so it's not a fluke. On only 42 midrange shots, though, the wobble is huge (about ±12 points for a 90% range), so a hot opponent midrange percentage over four games means very little.

Our rule of thumb: a gap more than twice the wobble is "too big to be a fluke." Even then, if you check 30 teams at once, one or two will clear that bar by luck, so an early result is a "watch this," not proof.

The math

For a percentage p based on n tries: wobble = √(p × (1 − p) ÷ n). For 100 coin flips: √(0.5 × 0.5 ÷ 100) = 0.05, or 5 points. Quadruple the tries and the wobble halves.

It's related to the standard deviation, which measures how spread out individual results are (one shot is either a make or a miss). The standard error is the standard deviation of an average or percentage: the standard deviation divided by √n.

"Twice the wobble" is close to the usual 95% line; 1.645 times the wobble gives a 90% range, which our charts often shade.

Orange, light orange, gray

How every chart on this site uses color

Color tells you how sure we are, and it means the same thing in every story.

Orange: the real signal. A change or gap bigger than normal chance wobble. Light orange: at the edge. At least 80% of the way to that bar: worth watching, not yet proven. Gray: noise. Within what luck alone could produce.

Other colors (like teal) are for comparison: last season, the league, another team. Every chart says what its colors mean underneath.

Correlation

Written as r

How closely two numbers move together, from 0 (not at all) to 1 (perfectly in step).

Each dot is one made-up player. The more the dots hug a line, the closer to 1.

In basketballA team's share of shots at the rim allowed in its first four games had a correlation of 0.74 with the rest of its season: defenses control where opponents shoot. Its opponents' 3-point percentage in those four games had a correlation of about 0 with the rest: that part is mostly luck.

Rough guide for our stories: below 0.2, the first number tells you almost nothing about the second; around 0.5, it's a useful hint; above 0.7, it's a good guide.

The math

This is Pearson's correlation. A correlation can also be negative (one goes up as the other goes down). It measures a straight-line relationship, and it doesn't say which causes which, or whether anything causes anything.

Totals, not averages

Rates like shooting percentage are computed from season totals, not by averaging each game's percentage.

In basketballA player goes 1-for-1 one night (100%) and 4-for-16 the next (25%). Averaging the two games says 62.5%. He actually made 5 of 17 shots: 29.4%. The 1-for-1 night shouldn't count as much as a 16-shot night, and totals take care of that.

Median and the middle half

The median is the middle value: half the players are above it, half below. The "middle half" is the range covering the middle 50% of players.

In basketballIf nine rookies improved by 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3 and 15 points per 36, the average is 3, pulled up by one breakout. The median is 2, which better describes the typical rookie. The middle half runs from 1 to 3.
The math

The middle half is the 25th to 75th percentile (the interquartile range). A wide middle half means players varied a lot.

Percentage points

Written as pp

The plain difference between two percentages.

In basketballA team going from 35% to 38% on threes improved by 3 percentage points (3 pp). Saying "3%" would be wrong: 3% of 35% is only about 1 point.

Per 36 minutes, per 100 possessions

Ways to compare players or teams fairly when they play different amounts or at different speeds.

In basketballPer 36: a bench player who scores 8 points in 18 minutes is scoring 16 per 36, the same rate as a starter who scores 16 in 36. Per 100 possessions: a fast team gets more trips down the floor, so it scores more points per game without being better; per 100 possessions removes that.
The math

Per 36 = 36 × points ÷ minutes. Possessions are estimated from the box score: shot attempts + 0.44 × free-throw attempts − offensive rebounds + turnovers.

Net rating

How many more points a team scores than it allows per 100 possessions. Zero is exactly average.

In basketball+5 means a team outscores opponents by 5 every 100 trips down the floor, a solid playoff team. Across the 2025-26 season, each point of net rating was worth about 2.2 wins over 82 games. A player's "on-court" net rating is the same idea, counting only the minutes he played; it's noisy, because it depends on his teammates and opponents too.

Effective FG% and true shooting

eFG% and TS%

Shooting percentages that give threes (and, for true shooting, free throws) their full value.

In basketballGoing 4-for-10 on threes scores 12 points; going 5-for-10 on twos scores 10. Plain FG% says the second is better (50% vs 40%); effective FG% says the first is (60% vs 50%). True shooting also counts free throws, so it's the best single measure of scoring efficiency. League average true shooting has been about 57–58% in recent seasons.
The math

eFG% = (made shots + 0.5 × made threes) ÷ shot attempts. TS% = points ÷ (2 × (shot attempts + 0.44 × free-throw attempts)).

Expected eFG%

What a set of shots "should" produce if each one went in at the league's rate for its spot on the floor.

In basketballA team whose opponents take lots of shots at the rim will allow a high expected eFG%, because rim shots go in most often. If opponents' actual eFG% is far above expected, they're making more than their locations predict: often luck, sometimes great shooters.

Ours uses location only. Private tracking models (like GeniusIQ, from the company behind Second Spectrum) also know how close the defender was and what kind of shot it was, so they're sharper. We say which one a story uses.

Usage

The share of his team's possessions a player finishes, with a shot, free throws or a turnover, while he's on the floor.

In basketballWith five players on the floor, about 20% is average. Stars who run the offense sit around 28–35%. Usage tells you how big a player's role is, not how well he plays it.

Regression

A way to compare things while holding something else steady.

In basketballOlder rookies tend to be drafted later. To ask whether age itself matters, we compared rookies taken at similar draft spots, so "older" doesn't just mean "picked later." A regression does that comparison for every draft spot at once.
The math

Our stories use ordinary least squares: it finds the straight-line relationship between, say, a rookie's growth and his age, after accounting for draft position. Each estimate comes with its own chance wobble.
