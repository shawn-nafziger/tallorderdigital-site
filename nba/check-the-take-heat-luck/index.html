Is Miami just unlucky?

Zach Kram says Miami's ugly start is partly bad luck and the Heat are bound to improve. His luck numbers are private, but the public shot data around them isn't. Here's what it shows.

By Shawn Nafziger · Shot data through Oct 27, the same games Kram used

The verdict

Holds up, on the part we can check

  • Tough shotsOpponents get to the rim far less against Miami (18.4% of their shots vs 27.9% league-wide). By location alone, that's the 5th-toughest shot diet in the league.
  • Hot shootingThey're making those shots anyway, +3.7 points of above what their locations predict. Over just 4 games, a gap that size is well within chance.
  • Will it last?Last season, how well opponents shot in a team's first 4 games said nothing about the rest of the season. Where they shot did.
  • Can't checkKram's luck numbers, and how closely each shot was contested.

The take

In ESPN's early-season overreactions piece (Oct 29), Zach Kram argues the 0–4 Heat are bound to improve: by GeniusIQ tracking they've had some of the league's worst shooting luck at both ends, and Miami is actually forcing opponents into tough shots, only for them to hit 53% from midrange and 40% from three (per Cleaning the Glass).

GeniusIQ is a private tracking model; we can't see it. But the shots themselves are public: every attempt, where it came from, and whether it went in. So we checked the parts of the take the public data can reach.

Miami keeps opponents away from the rim

Shots at the rim are the easiest in basketball. Against Miami, only 18.4% of opponents' shots came there, compared with 27.9% league-wide. On 369 shots, that share would normally wobble about ±2.3 points by chance, so a gap about 4 times that size is no fluke. More of their shots were threes and floaters instead.

If you value each shot at what the league made from that spot last season, opponents' shot diet against Miami was worth an of 52.9%, 5th-lowest in the league (the league average is 54.5%). That's location only, not how tightly each shot was guarded, but it points the same way as Kram's "tough shots."

Opponents are hot, but four games is very few

Opponents' actual eFG% against Miami was 56.5%, +3.7 points above what their locations predict, the 3rd-biggest gap in the league. On 42 midrange shots they made 59.5% (league: 40.0%), the hottest in the league; from three, 39.0% on 177 attempts. (Cleaning the Glass filters out garbage time and draws its zones differently, so its 53% and our 59.5% differ; both point the same way.)

But 42 shots is about ten a game. On that few, a team's opponent midrange percentage can swing about ±12 points by chance alone. Over the whole 2025-26 season, every team's opponent midrange percentage landed between 37.5% and 45.7%.

Hot opponent shooting doesn't last

The real question behind "bound to improve" is whether early opponent shooting tells you anything about later. Last season it didn't. A team's opponent midrange percentage over its first 4 games had a of -0.11 with the rest of its season; opponent 3-point percentage, -0.03. In plain terms: none.

Where opponents shoot is different. Opponents' share of shots at the rim carried over strongly (0.74). Defenses control where shots come from far more than whether they go in. So the part of Miami's start that's likely to last is the good part.

Kram's take holds up where public data can reach it: Miami is forcing tougher shots, opponents are making them anyway, and that kind of hot shooting hasn't lasted. The Heat's offense (4.1 points of eFG% below what its shot locations predict) is the same story in reverse.

Method

  • Data: every regular-season shot from the NBA Stats API via the open-source nba_api client: 2025-26, and 2026-27 through Oct 27 (4 Heat games), matching the games in Kram's article.
  • Expected eFG%: each shot is valued at the league's 2025-26 make rate for its zone (at the rim, rest of the paint, midrange, corner 3, above-the-break 3), times 1.5 for threes. It knows location, not defender distance, shot type or shooter.
  • Chance: the gap's comes from the shot-by-shot spread (3.3 points for Miami's opponents). The midrange swing is a 90% range around the league rate on 42 shots. With 30 defenses, two or three will land beyond 2 standard errors by chance alone, so a colored dot this early is a "watch this," not proof.
  • Carry-over: for each 2025-26 defense, its first 4 games vs the rest of its season, correlated across the 30 teams.
  • Not checked: GeniusIQ's shot-quality and luck model (private tracking data) and Cleaning the Glass's filtered splits; we cite them, we don't reproduce them.

Data: NBA Stats API via the open-source nba_api client. Not affiliated with or endorsed by the NBA or any team.
