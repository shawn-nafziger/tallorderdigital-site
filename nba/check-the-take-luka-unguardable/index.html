Is Luka unguardable?

Zach Lowe calls Luka Dončić the MVP favorite and "completely unguardable." Every number behind that is public, so we checked three of them: how much he carries, how well he shoots, and what happens when he sits.

By Shawn Nafziger · Data through Nov 6 (9 games for Dončić, 10 for the Lakers)

The take

On his podcast this week, Zach Lowe called Dončić the MVP favorite and "completely unguardable," pointing to his scoring (about 38.5 points a game at the time) and to the Lakers being +21 per 100 possessions with him on the floor and −17 with him off it (as reported by BasketballNews, Nov 6).

Then on Friday night Dončić scored 53 at Portland on 17-of-33 shooting, with 18 free-throw attempts. The Lakers are 8–2.

"Unguardable" is a judgment, not a stat. But it rests on three things we can measure, and they don't deserve the same trust after 9 games. How much a player carries settles in about four games. How well he shoots takes months. And how his team does with him off the floor is the noisiest number of all.

The workload isn't new. The shooting is

Dončić is scoring 40.1 points a game, up from 33.5 last season. His minutes haven't changed (35.9 a game vs 35.8). Neither, really, has his job: his is 39.7%, +2.9 points from last season, short of the 4-point move we'd call a new role. His job was already enormous.

So where do the extra 6.6 points come from? Split them into two parts. He's using a few more shooting chances a game (29.5, counting free-throw trips, vs 27.2), worth about 2.9 points at last season's efficiency. The other 3.7 points come from making more of them: his is 67.9%, up from 61.6% (league: 57.2%).

He makes shots an average player wouldn't

Not all shots are equal: a layup goes in about two times in three, a midrange jumper about two in five. So we value every shot he's taken at what the league makes from that spot this season. That's his : what an average shooter would make on the same shots. Dončić's 221 shots are worth +11.7 pp more than that. On that many shots, a gap that big would be a fluke well under 1 time in 20.

The midrange is where it shows most. He's made 54.8% of 42 midrange shots; the league makes 39.6%. Half his shots (51.1%) are above-the-break threes, where his 39.8% (league: 34.4%) is good but within chance on 113 attempts. That threes share is up from 47.6% of his shots from three last season to 56.1% now, a real change in his shot diet (2.4 times the wobble).

He also tops the league. Of the 78 qualified players with 100 or more shots, nobody beats what his shot locations predict by more. One honest caveat: with 78 players, about 3.5 would land beyond the luck line by chance alone, and 3 did: Dončić and two players running cold. A leaderboard this early is mostly noise. What makes Dončić's spot believable is last season: +5.7 pp on 1,457 shots, 3.6 times the wobble. He's a great shot-maker, and he was before this hot start.

Better than last year's Luka? Too early

The real question behind "unguardable" is whether this is a new level or a hot streak by an already great shooter. Compare him with himself: +11.7 pp now vs +5.7 pp last season is a gap of +6.0 pp. On 221 shots, his number would normally wobble about ±4.3 pp by chance alone, so a +6.0-pp gap is only 1.3 times the wobble. That's normal for a great shooter on a hot couple of weeks.

That's why the gray bar in the first chart is gray. About 3.7 of his extra points come from shooting better than his own normal, and that part could easily fade. The workload part won't.

The Lakers fall apart without him, but handle with care

Lowe's on/off numbers have held up. Through Friday the Lakers are +20.4 points per 100 possessions with Dončić on the floor and −19.7 without him, a 40.2-point gap. By the usual luck math that's 4.1 times the wobble, and he's third in the league.

Two reasons to treat it as the weakest of the three. First, the sample: only 319 Lakers possessions without him, about three games' worth, including all of the Oct 28 game he missed (a win over the Clippers). Second, on/off measures lineups, not one player: when a star sits, his team usually plays its bench, often against the other team's starters. That's why the list is crowded with role players right now. Alex Caruso, who finishes 12.1% of his team's plays, tops it at 51.1. And 35 of 309 qualified players clear the luck line, far more than the 14 chance would give, a sign the simple luck math understates how much on/off swings.

Lowe's take holds up where it matters: Dončić carries more of his offense than anyone and makes shots an average player wouldn't, and he did both last season too. What's not proven is the leap. The jump from 33.5 to 40.1 points a game leans on shooting that's within chance of his own normal, so expect something closer to last year's Luka. That's still the league's heaviest load, carried by an elite shot-maker.

Method

  • Data: NBA Stats API box scores and shot locations via the open-source nba_api client, 2025-26 and 2026-27 through Nov 6. The numbers are frozen at that date from the site's Player Profile and Hot and Cold Shooters dashboards, which use the same data and math.
  • Usage: the NBA's per-game usage (share of team plays a player finishes while on the floor), weighted by minutes. A move of 4+ points counts as a role change.
  • Where the points come from: points = 2 × true shooting × shooting chances (FGA + 0.44 × FTA), all from season totals. Volume = the change in chances at last season's true shooting; shooting = this season's chances times the change in true shooting.
  • Shot making: actual eFG% minus expected eFG%, where each shot is valued at its own season's league make rate for its zone (rim, rest of paint, midrange, corner 3, above-the-break 3). It knows location, not defender distance or shot type. Its comes from the shot-by-shot spread (4.3 pp this season, 1.6 last season); the gap with himself combines both (4.6 pp). Beyond luck = more than 2 standard errors.
  • On/off: on = 100 × his plus-minus ÷ his possessions; off = the Lakers' point differential without him, per 100 of their possessions without him. Luck band from a 14.4-point single-game spread in net rating. It doesn't adjust for teammates, opponents or garbage time, so it's shown as context, not proof.
  • Not checked: how closely each shot was contested (NBA tracking has defender-distance splits we haven't pulled yet) and private shot-quality models.
