Ready Now

Yaxel Lendeborg is a 24-year-old rookie playing star minutes. Older rookies usually get smaller jobs and grow less. Here's how unusual his start is, and what five games can and can't tell us.

Star minutes, unusual age

The Warriors took Lendeborg 11th out of Michigan at 24. The typical lottery pick in the last 6 drafts was 20 as a rookie. Through 5 games he's playing 33.6 minutes a night.

Rookies almost never play that much. Of the 214 rookies who played 40+ games in the last 6 seasons, 17 averaged 30+ minutes. The oldest was 22, and 13 of the 17 were top-5 picks. In those six drafts only 6 lottery picks were 23 or older, and the most minutes any of them averaged was 28.0.

Smaller jobs, less growth

Age doesn't decide who plays. First-round rookies reach real minutes (500+ for the season) at about the same rate at every age: 79% for 19- and 20-year-olds, 75% at 21–22, 77% at 23 and older.

What changes is the job. Older first-rounders use a smaller share of their team's possessions (16.9% vs 19.4%) and shoot about as efficiently. They arrive as role players.

And they grow less. By year 3, the typical 19- or 20-year-old rookie had added +2.8 points per 36; the typical rookie aged 23 or older, +0.6. Comparing players taken at similar draft spots (a ), each extra year of age at entry meant about 0.5 fewer points per 36 of growth.

That's what makes Lendeborg's start unusual twice over: star minutes, and a bigger share of the offense (21.2%) than the typical young first-rounder gets.

Points aren't the whole picture, so we checked 7 measures. Younger rookies improved more in every one. Their shooting efficiency rose about twice as much (+3.5 vs +1.7 percentage points of ), so they didn't just get more shots; they got better.

Each stat on its own is noisier than points. Only the scoring gap is clearly beyond normal noise, and steals plus blocks is close. The rest lean the same way but are too noisy to call one by one. The consistency is the point: 7 measures, one direction.

What five games can tell us

Five games is very little, but it isn't nothing. Last season's rookies show which part of a fast start to believe. Their minutes in their first five games tracked their minutes the rest of the season closely ( 0.77). Their scoring didn't (0.33).

So Lendeborg's role is real: the Warriors are giving him big minutes, and they're likely to keep doing it. His 24.2 points per 36 on 57.2% is a promising start, not yet a level.

History says a 24-year-old rookie arrives as a role player and doesn't change much. Five games in, Lendeborg's minutes say otherwise. Watch whether the scoring holds.

Method

  • Data: NBA Stats API season totals, 2020-21 to 2025-26 and 2026-27 through Oct 28, with each player's age, debut season and draft pick, via the open-source nba_api client.
  • Rookie = a player's first season with an NBA appearance. Age is the age the NBA reports for that season.
  • Rates come from totals: points per 36 = 36 × points ÷ minutes; true shooting = points ÷ (2 × (FGA + 0.44 × FTA)); usage is weighted by minutes.
  • Growth: change in points per 36 from year 1 to year 3, for players with 500+ minutes in both seasons. The "per extra year of age" figure is a regression of that change on age at entry and draft position; it's about twice its standard error (0.24), so a modest but real effect.
  • Who's missing from the growth numbers: rookies who didn't reach 500 minutes in year 3. That rate was similar across ages (58–62% of first-rounders played 1,000+ minutes in year 3), so it doesn't tilt the comparison much.
  • Limits: six rookie classes, and only 6 lottery picks aged 23+, so comparisons for the oldest high picks rest on few players. Points per 36 measures volume, not all-around impact.
