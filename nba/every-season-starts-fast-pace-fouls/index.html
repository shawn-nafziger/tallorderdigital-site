Every season starts fast

The first 13 days of 2026-27 looked like the fastest, most whistle-happy basketball in years. Next to the same 13 days of the last five seasons, they look ordinary, and openings like this have faded every year.

Fast starts are normal

Early reports called this the fastest pace in about 40 years (theScore). Through Nov 1, teams are averaging 101.3 possessions per 48 minutes. That's higher than any full season in our five seasons of history (the fastest was 100.9).

But that compares two weeks with whole seasons. Last season's first 13 days ran at 102.4, 1.2 possessions faster than this season's. That gap is about 1.6 times the normal chance wobble: at the edge, not quite enough to call this start slower for sure.

Fast openings fade. In each of the last five seasons, the pace slowed after the first 13 days, by 0.7 to 2.0 possessions a game, and in four of the five the drop was too big to be a fluke. Last season went from 102.4 to 100.8.

A few more whistles, not more free throws

Before the season, the league told referees to call off-arm push-offs, illegal screens and contact away from the ball more tightly, and said to expect more whistles early (Bleacher Report). Fouls are up a little: 22.3 per 100 possessions, the most of any opening in six seasons, against 21.7 a year ago. That gap of 0.5 is about 1.2 times the normal chance wobble. To count as real, it would need to be about 0.9.

Free throws aren't up. Teams are taking 24.8 free throws per 100 possessions, down from 25.3, and the (FTA ÷ FGA) is .286, down from .295. More fouls with fewer free throws would fit a crackdown on offensive fouls, such as push-offs and illegal screens, which never send anyone to the line. Checking that needs play-by-play data we haven't pulled, so for now it's a hint, not a finding.

And early whistles fade. In each of the last two seasons, fouls fell by more than 2 per 100 possessions after the first 13 days (2.3 in 2024-25, 2.3 in 2025-26), too much to be a fluke both times.

The three-point share is the number that sticks

Threes are the exception. So far 42.8% of shots are threes, the highest opening share of the six seasons, though only 0.8 above last year's 42.0%, within chance wobble.

Unlike pace and fouls, the opening share hardly moves over the rest of the season: by less than 1 point in every one of the last five seasons. How many threes a team takes is a choice coaches make, and it shows up right away (more in The Shot Diet).

Judge a new season against the same days of past seasons, not against whole seasons. By that measure, 2026-27 isn't faster than last year, and its whistles are within chance of last year's. We'll check again around game 20.

All five numbers, every season

Method

  • Data: every regular-season team box score from the NBA Stats API via the open-source nba_api client: 2021-22 to 2025-26, and 2026-27 through Nov 1.
  • Windows: "the first 13 days" runs from each season's opening night through the same number of days 2026-27 has had (Oct 20 to Nov 1 this season). Each opening window has 91 to 95 games. "The rest of the season" is every later game.
  • Rates come from , never averages of per-game rates. Possessions are estimated from the box score (FGA + 0.44 × FTA − offensive rebounds + turnovers, averaged over both teams), the same formula for every season. That estimate runs about 1.1% above the NBA's own count, so it's scaled to match the NBA's count over the full 2025-26 season. The scaling is the same for every season, so it can't change a comparison.
  • Fouls and free throws are per team per 100 possessions. Free-throw rate here is FTr = FTA ÷ FGA, not FT ÷ FGA.
  • Chance: each window's comes from the game-to-game spread. A gap counts as real when it's more than 2 standard errors of the difference, and "at the edge" at 1.6.
  • Not checked: which kinds of fouls are up (that needs play-by-play), and why pace slows after the opening.
