How much is a billion?

Most of us file a million and a billion in the same mental drawer, labeled "a lot." Before we show you anything, take a guess. Then see the gap as money, time and real things.

By Shawn Nafziger · Published Nov 14, 2026 · Figures from 2025 and 2026

A million sits a tenth of one percent of the way to a billion. On a phone screen, that's about one pixel from zero.

If your dot landed far to the right, you're in large company. In the 2013 study, by David Landy, Noah Silbert and Aleah Goldin, about half of 400 American adults put a million about 43% of the way along a line from a thousand to a billion. The researchers think many of us treat "thousand, million, billion" as evenly spaced steps, like rungs on a ladder, when each rung is really a thousand times higher than the last. (A smaller first experiment, with 64 college students and graduates, found the same pattern in about 35% of them.)

How to read these A million is 1,000,000 (six zeros). A is 1,000,000,000 (nine zeros), a thousand millions. A trillion is a thousand billions. Every chart below uses a , where equal distances mean equal amounts, except the last, which says so. Orange is always the number the chart is about. Gray is everything else.

How long is a billion seconds?

Time works the same way. A million seconds is about 11.6 days, a long vacation. A billion seconds is about 31.7 years, most of a career. A trillion seconds is about 31,700 years, longer than people have been writing things down.

What comes close to a billion?

Here are real things measured in , the whole first grid as one square. Everyone living in the U.S. (341.8 million people) fills about a third of one. Every trip through a TSA checkpoint in 2025 (906.7 million) and every boarding on a U.S. airline (972 million) each come just shy of a full square. Only the world's population, about 8.23 billion, and the 2026 midterms' political ads, about $11.21 billion spent or reserved by late October, run past it.

Where does your number fit?

Seen a big number in the news? Type it in, with or without the word: "4.2 billion", "750 million", "3,500,000".

Or try:

if it were seconds
if it were heartbeats, at 70 a minute
if it were dollars, at $10,000 a day

Orange: your number. Gray: the landmarks above. This ruler uses a : each step is 1,000 times the one before, so a million, a billion and a trillion look evenly spaced. That's the shortcut our minds take. The charts above show the real gaps.

A billion is a thousand millions. The next time a news story says "million" and "billion" in the same sentence, picture a single square and the whole grid.

Method

  • The study: Landy, D., Silbert, N., & Goldin, A. (2013). Estimating large numbers. Cognitive Science, 37(5), 775–799. Its main experiment had 400 U.S. adults recruited online (Amazon Mechanical Turk), not a random sample of Americans. Their line ran from 1 thousand to 1 billion; ours starts at zero. A million sits at about 0.1% on both.
  • $10,000 a day: today's payment is day 1, so the millionth dollar arrives on day 100 and the billionth on day 100,000. The dates use your device's calendar and skip nothing for leap years.
  • Seconds and heartbeats: a year is 365.25 days. Heartbeats assume a steady 70 beats a minute, so a billion beats takes about 27 years.
  • Landmarks: U.S. population on July 1, 2025 (Census Bureau, Vintage 2025 estimates). TSA checkpoint screenings in 2025, all 365 daily counts added up (TSA). Passengers on U.S. airlines in 2025, domestic and international, 12 monthly figures added up (Bureau of Transportation Statistics). World population in mid-2025 (United Nations, World Population Prospects 2024; a ). Political ad spending in the 2026 midterms, spent or reserved as of Oct 29, 2026 (an estimate by the ad-tracking firm AdImpact, in ; all races and parties combined).
  • Readers' guesses: each guess is counted anonymously in a rough range (for example "10–20% along"), with no cookies. A "readers so far" line appears here once at least 300 people have guessed in a round.
